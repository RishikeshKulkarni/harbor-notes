The five value-creation pillars (EGF's own framing)

  1. Strategic guidance — board-level direction and thesis sharpening
  2. Institutionalization & governance — turning founder-led companies into board-led ones

04Job-to-be-done

The role: Chief of Staff & PMO inside Value Creation

This is not a "manage Karolos's calendar" role. At a fund this young, this seat owns the operating system of Portfolio Operations. What that means concretely:

  1. Leadership material. Prep Board, IC (Investment Committee) and ExCo (Executive Committee) decks. Translate Giacomo's portfolio diagnostics + Karolos's strategic framing into 6-page documents (not 60-slide decks).
  2. Cross-portfolio visibility. One source of truth for what is happening across Tarmeem, CarniStore, and every future portfolio company — value-creation plan progress, KPI movement, board issues, escalations.
  3. Cadence design. Weekly portfolio review. Monthly portfolio-company scorecard. Quarterly portfolio board pack. 100-day-plan tracking for each new investment.
  4. Prioritization. When the Value Creation team has 9 portfolio companies and 30 levers, which 5 get attention this quarter? You frame the trade-off.
  5. Stakeholder management. Karolos owns four teams (Value Creation, Partnerships, PR, PMO). You are the connective tissue. You will spend real time with the EDB and ministry interfaces.
  6. NCP (National Champions Programme) secretariat. The NCP is a trilateral with MoIAT (Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology) and MoET (Ministry of Economy and Tourism) — it needs PMO discipline or it collapses into a PR exercise.
  7. Decision-velocity engine. A good PMO speeds decisions; a bad one creates status reports. Be clear on which kind you are.
  8. Talent-placement support. EGF's value-creation pillar #5 is talent — you'll likely orchestrate operator placements into portfolio companies.
  9. Exit-readiness rhythm. Even at year 1, exit posture starts. Data rooms, audit-readiness, governance hygiene.
  10. Be the operator they trust with the awkward conversation. The unsaid sentence in every Chief of Staff job description.

08Useful background — not for asking

Hidden context: the BGF (Business Growth Fund) lineage

EGF is structurally a UAE adaptation of the UK's BGF (Business Growth Fund), a £3bn growth-equity platform launched in 2011 by the major UK banks alongside the government. The evidence:

  • Stephen Welton — founder of BGF — sits on the EGF board.
  • Matthew Singh — EGF's CIO — came directly from BGF, where he led investments from 2015.
  • Same playbook: government/bank-backed, minority equity, "patient capital", growth-stage SMEs, sector-priority focus, governance-first value creation.
  • BGF is the world's most-active growth investor by deal volume — over 600 investments since 2011 — so the model is battle-tested.
How to handle this in the interview

Don't lead with it and don't ask Karolos about it as a "smart question" — at the Chief of Staff / PMO level he's hiring an operator, not a fund-strategy analyst, and structural lineage questions can read as "I'm evaluating you back."

BUT — keep it in your back pocket. If he himself mentions BGF, Stephen Welton, Matthew, or the model lineage, you can say: "Yes — I noticed Stephen's on the board and Matthew came from there. The active-minority + sector-priority pattern reads very BGF-flavoured. Is that a deliberate model choice or did the team converge there independently?" That's an "if it comes up" question, not a planted one.

09What Karolos will ask

Likely questions — with full answers

Each question below gives you (1) literally what to say, (2) why it works at this seat, and (3) the pitfall to avoid. Memorize the shape, not the words.

  1. Walk me through your background.

    There's a throughline in my career that maps directly onto this seat. I'm an operator who's spent the last [N] years [building / scaling / fixing] [P&L-shaped thing]. The shift in the middle of my career was from owning one P&L (Profit & Loss) to orchestrating across several — which is when I learned that translating principal intent into portfolio-wide execution is its own discipline. That's the work I want to do at EGF.

    Why

    Karolos is BCG-trained. He listens for structure. Opening with the throughline (operator → orchestrator) and then plugging into the seat tells him in 30 seconds: "this person can sequence." A chronological CV recital ("First I worked at…") loses him by sentence two.

    Avoid

    Filling 5 minutes. Aim for 90 seconds. Land it and pause — let him direct where he wants the depth.

  2. Why EGF? Why now?

    Three reasons, in order. First, the stage: EGF is one year old this month — Portfolio Operations is being wired, not run. Joining at the operating-system layer is rare. Second, the mandate: this isn't a generic growth fund chasing returns — it's an industrial-policy instrument laddering up to Operation 300bn and ICV. The "why" is unusually clear. Third, the seat: a Chief of Staff & PMO sitting at the intersection of investment thesis and operating discipline is the role I've been building toward. Either side alone is common — both is the multiplier.

    Why

    Three structured reasons = BCG points. Each reason is about EGF, not you. The closing line ("both is the multiplier") names what makes the role different — that's the framing Karolos uses internally.

    Avoid

    "I love growth funds" or "I want to work in the UAE." Both are about you. Don't say either.

  3. What does a Chief of Staff actually do, in your view?

    Three things, and they're all multipliers — not administration. First, force multiplier for the principal: I take what's in your head and make it real across four teams without you having to repeat yourself. Second, decision accelerant: I shorten the time between a decision needing to be made and it actually being made — that means clear pre-reads, clear options, clear owners. Third, discreet operator: I handle the awkward conversation you don't have time to have, and I never make you find out from someone else. The PMO is the tool I use to do all of this — it's not the job.

    Why

    Karolos will hire the candidate who explicitly rejects the gatekeeper / calendar-manager framing. The "PMO is a tool, not the job" line is quote-worthy — say it. "Decision accelerant" is the highest-status framing of this role; it tells him you understand the seat's leverage.

    Avoid

    Talking about meetings, calendars, prioritizing his inbox. That's an EA (Executive Assistant), not a Chief of Staff.

  4. How is value creation different from consulting?

    Skin in the game and time horizon. A consultant leaves at the end of the slide deck — value creation lives with the consequences for three to seven years. That changes everything. The portfolio CEO is your colleague, not your client. You're accountable for the EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, Amortization) line, not the recommendation. And you sit through the J-curve with them — which builds a different kind of trust than a project relationship ever can. That's the part of the work I find most interesting.

    Why

    Karolos was at BCG for a decade. He was the consultant. He's testing whether you understand the actual delta — which is ownership and time, not vocabulary. The "colleague not client" frame lands because it's how he now experiences the work.

    Avoid

    Trashing consultants. He was one. Show you understand the value of structured thinking — but make the point that ownership is the differentiator.

  5. Tell me about a portfolio company (or business unit) you helped turn around.

    [Your story — fill in with one specific case. Structure it as: Situation (one sentence on what was broken — name the metric), Lever (the one thing you changed, with mechanism), Resistance (who pushed back and how you handled it — don't skip this), Outcome (the metric, with a number, and the time horizon).] Total: 90 seconds.

    Why

    Karolos will judge this on active verbs, numbers, and whether you mention resistance. Most candidates skip resistance because it's uncomfortable — but the resistance moment is where the operator-vs-consultant distinction shows. Name what was hard, name what you did about it.

    Avoid

    "We drove alignment." "We aligned stakeholders." Passive-voice consulting verbs. Replace with: "I sat with the CFO every Friday for six weeks until the cash forecast was clean."

  6. You'd be supporting Giacomo's Value Creation roadmap AND running PMO discipline across Partnerships and PR. How do you actually prioritize across those?

    Three mechanisms. First, WIP (Work In Progress) limits: at any moment I'd cap the active portfolio-company initiatives — say four to six — and force the team to finish before starting. Second, escalation triggers: explicit, written rules for when something pops to you versus when I hold it. "If a portfolio CEO is two weeks past a commitment, that's mine. If it's two months, that's yours." Third, a weekly 15-minute reframe with you where we look at what's in flight, what should drop, and what's missing. The prioritization isn't the hard part — the discipline to actually cut things is.

    Why

    This question is testing whether you have mechanisms or just opinions. Naming WIP limits, escalation triggers, and a recurring reframe sounds like an operator who has run this before. The throwaway line "the discipline to actually cut things" signals you've felt the failure mode.

    Avoid

    Naming a tool (Asana, Monday, Notion, Linear). Junior. Name a mechanism, not software.

  7. A portfolio CEO is stonewalling the value-creation playbook. What do you do?

    First move: diagnose. Is this intent or capacity? Intent means they disagree with the playbook — capacity means they agree but can't execute. Different problems. If it's intent: I'd schedule a 1:1 without the deck, get genuinely curious about their thesis on what's blocking growth, and look for the overlap between their view and ours. If it's capacity: find the missing resource — a CFO, a head of commercial, a fractional operator. What I would not do is go around the CEO to the board on the first move. That's a one-shot weapon and you only use it when nothing else worked.

    Why

    "Intent versus capacity" is a senior diagnostic. Most candidates jump to action; you're framing the problem first. The "go around the CEO to the board" comment shows you understand portfolio governance politics — that single sentence buys you a lot of credibility.

    Avoid

    "I'd present the data and convince them." Founders aren't moved by decks — they're moved by trust, incentives, and being heard. The portfolio CEO has their investors too — never forget you're a minority holder.

  8. Walk me through IRR vs MOIC vs DPI. Which matters most here?

    They tell different stories to different audiences. IRR (Internal Rate of Return) is time-weighted — it makes a 2x in two years look much better than a 3x in seven. It's the LP (Limited Partner) presentation number. MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital) is the absolute return — total value over cost — and it ignores time. DPI (Distributions to Paid-In) is the only one that's actually cash returned. For EGF specifically: this is patient capital with a federal backer, you're explicitly not optimizing IRR — so MOIC and TVPI (Total Value to Paid-In) are the right reportables in the J-curve years, and DPI is the truth at exit. The fund's brand promise — "capital that grows with you" — only works if you're MOIC-led, not IRR-led.

    Why

    This is the most technical question Karolos will ask, and the highest-leverage one to get exactly right. The connection from "patient capital" to "not optimizing IRR" is the move — most candidates name the three metrics but don't tie them to EGF's actual posture.

    Avoid

    Treating them as interchangeable. Or worse — saying "IRR is what matters" because that's the default PE answer. It's wrong for this fund.

  9. How would you build a 100-day plan for the next EGF acquisition?

    Three phases, in order: stabilize, lock, set. Days 1–30 stabilize: get cash forecasting clean, get monthly reporting on rails, lock the top relationships — bank, auditor, key customers — and make sure no one is leaving in the first 90 days who shouldn't. Days 30–60 lock: identify three value levers, no more. Each lever gets a metric, an owner, and a date. Days 60–100 set: install the operating cadence — weekly portfolio review with management, monthly scorecard to us, quarterly board pack. Strategy comes last, not first. CarniStore is probably still in this window — I'd want to know where it is in the arc.

    Why

    The order is the answer. Most candidates lead with strategy — wrong. Stabilize-lock-set is the proven sequence and naming it that way signals you've actually done a 100-day plan before. Closing with "CarniStore is probably in this window" shows live-fire awareness of the portfolio.

    Avoid

    Strategy in days 1–30. Listing every workstream. A 100-day plan with 12 workstreams is a 0-day plan.

  10. What's a PMO you'd actually want to work in?

    A decision-velocity engine, not a status-report factory. Three signals of a good one: it kills more dashboards than it creates; the principal answers the phone on the first ring when the PMO lead calls; and the PMO is a little bit feared — because it's the function that asks the awkward question other people don't want to. If I'm in this seat and a year from now the team is generating more decisions per week and fewer slides per week than today, the PMO is working.

    Why

    "Decision-velocity engine, not status-report factory" is the line. Say it verbatim. The three signals make it concrete — and "the PMO is a little bit feared" is the senior-operator detail that most candidates won't say out loud.

    Avoid

    "I love processes." Dead on arrival.

  11. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a principal or your CEO.

    [Your story.] Anchor the shape: I disagreed in private, brought data not opinion, gave them a clean option set rather than a single recommendation, and once they decided I committed in public — even when the decision wasn't the one I preferred. The outcome doesn't have to be "I was right." That's a brittle story. The stronger story is: we disagreed, the decision got made, the outcome wasn't perfect, and the trust survived.

    Why

    "Disagree in private, commit in public" is the senior frame. Karolos has worked alongside the ADNOC CEO and the EDB board — he knows what mature disagreement looks like. Picking a story where you "won by being loud" tells him you can't handle the room.

    Avoid

    A story where you were right and the principal was wrong, dramatized for effect. He'll smell it.

  12. How do you coordinate across teams when you don't have direct authority?

    Three things make this work. First, I make myself useful before I need to be useful — I do the unglamorous work (clean pre-reads, follow-ups, getting the right people in the room) so that when I ask for something, I've already banked credit. Second, I keep escalation visible — the threat of going to the principal is a tool that loses its power the moment you actually use it; I rarely use it, which is why it works. Third, I orient around the principal's actual priorities, not the org chart — if Karolos cares about a portfolio company this quarter, that's what gets coordinated, regardless of which team formally owns it.

    Why

    This is THE core question for a Chief of Staff role and it's almost always asked. The three principles — be useful first, escalation as a sheathed weapon, orient on the principal — are senior. Most candidates answer this with "I build relationships" — vague and unactionable.

    Avoid

    "I'm a strong communicator." Everyone says this. It's noise.

  13. How do you handle a portfolio company that's missing its plan?

    It depends on how it's missing. Three modes. Pace miss — revenue is growing but slower than projected: that's a forecasting problem, not a business problem; recalibrate the plan and move on. Lever miss — a specific initiative isn't working: diagnose whether it's the lever or the execution; if the lever is wrong, kill it before the team gets attached. Trajectory miss — the business model itself is broken: that's a board conversation, not a PMO conversation, and the right move is to surface it early and clean. The mistake junior PMOs make is treating all three the same way and putting everything in the same "amber" status.

    Why

    Three modes = structure. Naming "the same amber status" failure mode shows you've watched a real PMO turn into a process theatre. The "kill the lever before the team gets attached" line is operator language.

    Avoid

    Generic "deep dive, root cause analysis, action plan" answers. That's a McKinsey deck, not a PMO move.

  14. What would your first 90 days in this seat look like?

    Same shape I'd use for a portfolio company. Days 1–30 listen: 1:1s with you, Giacomo, Matthew, the Partnerships and PR leads, the Tarmeem and CarniStore boards. Read every IC memo, every board pack, every value-creation plan that exists. Don't propose anything. Days 30–60 diagnose: write a 4-page memo for you on what the operating system actually looks like today versus what it needs to look like in 12 months. Identify three things that are broken and three things that are working but invisible. Days 60–90 install: a weekly portfolio review cadence, a monthly portfolio-company scorecard format, the NCP secretariat structure. Pick one thing to ship — not five. Ship it cleanly.

    Why

    This is almost certainly going to be asked. Mirroring the 100-day plan structure (listen-diagnose-install) signals consistency. "Don't propose anything in the first 30 days" is the senior move — most candidates pitch reforms in the first week.

    Avoid

    A 90-day plan with 10 deliverables. A plan with too many things in it tells Karolos you'd be a status-report factory.

  15. How would you build trust with a portfolio CEO in the first 30 days post-investment?

    Show up useful, not impressive. The portfolio CEO has just taken our money — they're nervous about loss of control, about reporting burden, about whether we're going to be a partner or a babysitter. In the first 30 days I'd do three concrete things. First, ask them "what's one thing I can take off your plate this month?" and actually do it — not promise to do it. Second, do not launch a new reporting template in week one; instead, take whatever they're already producing and translate it into what we need. Third, never let them be surprised by anything coming from us — every escalation, every board ask, every Karolos visit they hear from me first. Trust is built in those three habits.

    Why

    Most candidates answer this with "relationship building." You're being concrete: take work off their plate, don't impose templates day one, no surprises. Each one is testable behaviour. Karolos has watched young investment professionals burn portfolio CEOs with exactly the opposite behaviours.

    Avoid

    Talking about "alignment workshops" or "kickoff offsites." The portfolio CEO has a business to run.

  16. What's your weakest area going into this role?

    [Pick one that's genuinely true and is a stretch but learnable — and that doesn't disqualify you from the seat.] Two good shapes: "I haven't worked inside a fund structure before — the IC dynamics, the LP layer, the fund-level economics versus the portfolio level. I've ramped on adjacent things quickly but I want to be honest that's the steepest part of the curve for me." Or: "I've worked across functions but not specifically in PR / Partnerships at a sovereign-adjacent fund — there's a UAE-government-interface skill I'd be building from scratch, and that's the part I'd lean on you and the team for in the first six months."

    Why

    The answer that lands is specific, learnable, and sized to a real gap. The two shapes above name a real gap, signal you've thought about it, and give Karolos a concrete picture of where he'd need to invest in you.

    Avoid

    "I'm a perfectionist." "I care too much." Lazy. Karolos has heard these from every candidate. Also avoid naming something that disqualifies you from the seat (e.g., "I'm not great at managing multiple stakeholders").

  17. Why should we hire YOU for this seat, specifically?

    Three things, briefly. One, I've sat on both sides of the operator-orchestrator line — I know what it feels like to be the portfolio CEO getting a value-creation call and the operator making that call. Two, I'm joining at the operating-system layer and that's what I'm best at — I get more done in the first 90 days of an unwritten role than in year two of a defined one. Three, I want to do this work specifically — not "a Chief of Staff role somewhere", this one, with this team, at this fund, at this moment. The match between the seat and what I do well is unusually tight.

    Why

    Three reasons, ranked. Specificity in the closer ("not 'a Chief of Staff role somewhere'") is what differentiates you. The "operating-system layer" framing tells Karolos you understand why this is the right moment to hire you.

    Avoid

    Listing skills. Listing accomplishments. The answer is about fit, not credentials — credentials got you in the room.

  18. What questions do you have for me?

    Pivot to §10. Pick two or three, not ten. Rank by which one most invites him to tell you something.

    Why

    The right question is the one that gets him talking — that's how the last ten minutes turn into a conversation rather than a Q&A.

10Your move

Questions to ask Karolos — validated for this seat

These are role-appropriate. You're being hired as an operator, so the strongest questions are about the work — cadence, decisions, bottlenecks, what success looks like — not about the fund's strategy, which would read as "I'm interviewing you back." Pick two or three; the first five are the sharpest.

  1. If I'm sitting in this seat 12 months from now and it's obviously working, what does that look like to you?

    Why this lands: It pulls Karolos into describing his actual vision for the seat, which tells you (a) what he'll measure you on, (b) what's missing from the org today, and (c) whether his answer is grounded or aspirational. The "obviously working" phrasing forces specifics. If he can't answer it concretely, that's information too.

  2. Where's the bottleneck in Portfolio Operations today — what's the work that has piled up because there's no PMO seat in place yet?

    Why this lands: Assumes you're being hired because something is broken or missing — which is true. Demonstrates you understand they're not just filling an org-chart slot. His answer is the implicit job description.

  3. How do you want decisions to flow between you, Giacomo's Value Creation team, and the portfolio-company CEOs? Where's the line you want me to hold versus escalate?

    Why this lands: Directly asks about authority and escalation, which is the single hardest part of a Chief of Staff role. Karolos will respect the candidate who knows to ask this before day one rather than blunder into it. His answer is also a tell on how clear his own thinking is yet.

  4. What does your time-mix look like today across Value Creation, Partnerships, PR, and PMO — and where do you wish it shifted?

    Why this lands: Reveals what he wants to spend more time on and, by implication, what he wants you to absorb. This is the most useful single answer you can extract from him for understanding the actual job.

  5. Sheikh Hamdan's Dubai private-sector agentic AI mandate landed on 4 May — two-year horizon. How does that filter into the Value Creation playbook for portfolio companies like CarniStore? Are you adding an AI-readiness lens to the value-creation pillars, or treating it as portfolio-specific for now?

    Why this lands: Most recent UAE government move that directly touches EGF's portfolio (Dubai-HQ portfolio companies have a hard deadline). Asking it shows you read this morning, not last week. The structural framing — "add to the pillars vs. treat as portfolio-specific" — gives Karolos an easy on-ramp to talk shop. Surfaces a topic he is statistically very likely thinking about but hasn't necessarily formed a public position on yet — which means he gets to think out loud with you. That's the best possible mode for the last ten minutes of an interview.

  6. When you've worked with Chief-of-Staff types before — at ADNOC, at BCG, at EDB — what's the most common failure mode you've seen?

    Why this lands: References his specific career (ADNOC + BCG + EDB), which signals you did the work. Asks him to name failure patterns, which is more useful than asking "what does success look like." You'll learn exactly what he's screening against.

  7. Where does Giacomo's team end and the portfolio-company management begin, in practice — not on paper?

    Why this lands: The "in practice, not on paper" phrasing is senior. It signals you know the org chart is the polite fiction and the actual line is fuzzier. Karolos's answer tells you whether the Value Creation team is hands-on operators or board-level advisors — those are very different jobs.

  8. What's the operating cadence in Portfolio Operations today — weekly portfolio review, monthly scorecards, quarterly boards — and where is it weakest?

    Why this lands: Asks about the actual fabric of the job. The "where is it weakest" tail makes it impossible to answer with "we have everything in place" — forces a real diagnostic from him.

  9. How does the NCP reshape your operating model for the next 18 months? Is it changing how the IC sources, or how Portfolio Operations engages?

    Why this lands: References the most recent, biggest thing they did (MIITE-week launch) without making it about strategy. The split (IC sourcing vs. Portfolio Operations engagement) is the right operator-level frame.

  10. What's the hardest part of building Portfolio Operations from scratch that you didn't expect when you took the role?

    Why this lands: Invites him to be candid. People love this question because it gives them permission to vent a little. His answer is a window into where the real friction sits — and quite possibly into the work you'll inherit.

  11. In a one-year-old fund with two live portfolio companies, what's the right size for the Value Creation team — and where are you in that build?

    Why this lands: Shows you're thinking about org design, not just your individual seat. Tells him you're going to be useful as a partner in building the function, not just executing inside it.

Picking on the spot

If you only have time for two: ask Q01 (12-month "obviously working") and either Q03 (decision flow / escalation line) or Q05 (agentic AI mandate). Q03 wins if Karolos is being precise/operational. Q05 wins if he's been animated and forward-looking — the AI question gives him the most room to riff and is the freshest data point in your bag.

11Don't step on these

Credibility traps

Junior says

"IRR is what matters."

"We need to set up KPIs for the portfolio."

"I'd run a workshop with the portfolio-company CEO."

"The PMO will track the value-creation projects."

"Value creation means growing the business."

You say

"IRR is for LP optics, MOIC for absolute return, DPI is what's actually returned. They tell different stories."

"Different signals matter: leading operating metrics, value drivers, reporting KPIs. CPOs notice when they're conflated."

"Portfolio-company CEOs want a thesis and a resource, not a workshop. I'd diagnose intent versus capacity first."

"The PMO accelerates decisions. Tracking is a byproduct, not the product."

"Value creation is offensive — revenue, margin, multiple. Value protection is defensive — governance, downside, compliance. Conflating them signals you've never sat on a portfolio-company board."

Calibrated humility

If Karolos asks something you genuinely don't know — about UAE federal structure, an obscure metric, a portfolio company — say so cleanly. "I don't know — what should I be reading?" beats invention every time. He has been an advisor to the ADNOC CEO; he will spot bluffing instantly.

12Speak the language

Operating vocabulary

IRR (Internal Rate of Return)
Time-weighted return on invested capital. Optics-heavy; penalizes long holds.
MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital)
Total return divided by cost. Patient-capital favourite.
TVPI (Total Value to Paid-In)
Includes unrealized value. The main reportable for a year-1 fund.
DPI (Distributions to Paid-In)
Cash actually returned to LPs. The only one that pays bills.
RVPI (Residual Value to Paid-In)
The "still in the ground" piece — TVPI minus DPI.
J-curve
The early dip in fund returns before exits start. Year-1 EGF lives here.
100-day plan
The post-close stabilize-and-set-cadence playbook for a newly acquired portfolio company.
Value bridge
Decomposition of equity-value uplift into revenue growth, margin, multiple expansion, debt paydown.
EBITDA bridge
Period-over-period walk of EBITDA: volume, price, mix, cost, one-offs.
Run-rate synergies
Annualized cost or revenue synergies achievable once a programme is fully implemented.
NWC (Net Working Capital)
Receivables + inventory − payables. The most under-managed value lever in growth-stage portfolio companies.
ICV (In-Country Value)
ADNOC's localization mandate. This is the gate to the Industrial Resilience Agreement.
NCP (National Champions Programme)
The EGF + MoIAT + MoET initiative launched 6 May 2026.
Operation 300bn
UAE's strategy to grow industrial GDP to AED 300bn by 2031. The macro thesis EGF ladders to.
The "missing middle"
EGF's own term — SMEs too big for VC, too entrepreneurial for traditional PE. Use it back to him.
Active minority
A minority equity stake with structured value-creation engagement. Governance via board seats and reporting rights, not control.
WIP (Work In Progress) limit
A cap on how many initiatives are active at once. The operating-system version of "fewer, bigger, better."
IC (Investment Committee)
The body that approves new deals. EGF's IC includes the CEO, CIO, CPO and board representation.
LP (Limited Partner)
A capital provider to a fund. For EGF, EDB is effectively the LP layer.
Agentic AI
AI systems that act autonomously — noticing issues, contacting parties, executing tasks — beyond chatbots or basic automation. Mandated for Dubai's private sector within two years (announced 4 May 2026).
13Last 2 minutes

Closing move

What to say when he asks "Anything else?"

"One thing I want to leave you with — you're 12 months in, you've just launched the NCP, you have ADNOC offtake on the table, and Portfolio Operations is being wired up in real time. The Chief of Staff & PMO seat right now is less about running a process and more about building the operating system you'll run for the next decade. That's the work I want. I'd like to do it with you."

Then: shut up. Smile. Don't elaborate. Let him close.

After the call

Within 90 minutes: send a 4-line follow-up referencing one specific thing he said + one specific thing about Tarmeem, CarniStore or the NCP. No attachment. No deck. Just the note.
